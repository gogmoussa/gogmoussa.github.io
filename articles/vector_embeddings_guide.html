Why LLMs Think in Geometry: Visualizing Vector Embeddings from First Principles

How machines turn messy human ideas into map coordinates, why angles beat rulers, and how modern AI searches meaning in milliseconds.

By George Moussa
•
8 min interactive read
•
3 Live Simulators

Pull out your phone right now, open your Photos app, and search for "dog sleeping on the couch" or "sunset at the beach".

Stop and think about what happens next. You never named those image files. You never typed keyword tags onto them. Your camera only saved a raw grid of colored pixels. Yet in a fraction of a second, your phone pulls up the exact photo you took three summers ago.

How does a machine look at a grid of pixels—or a messy human sentence—and actually know what it means?

For a long time, every time I asked how AI search or memory worked, people threw the same buzzword at me: "Vector Embeddings." And whenever I tried to look it up, I got hit with walls of linear algebra formulas and arrays of 1,536 numbers. I didn't want a math textbook—I wanted to actually see how it works in my head.

The Core Secret (Zero Jargon):

Computers still don't understand words or feelings. What they do understand is geometry. An embedding is simply a trick that turns any idea—a word, a paragraph, a song, or a photo—into a GPS coordinate on a map where similar meanings live in the same neighborhood.

1. Building the Map: The Trait Sliders

How do you turn a concept into a map coordinate? Imagine you work at a restaurant and want a computer to understand your menu using just two trait sliders rated from 0 to 10:

  • Slider 1 (Horizontal X-Axis): Sweetness (0 = Savory, 10 = Pure Sugar)
  • Slider 2 (Vertical Y-Axis): Crunchiness (0 = Soft/Liquid, 10 = Super Crunchy)

If we rate an Apple, we give it Sweetness = 8 and Crunchiness = 9. That gives us a two-number coordinate: [8, 9]. A Pear gets [7, 7]. A Steak gets [1, 2].

Try it below: Drag the two sliders for your Mystery Food and watch how the computer automatically knows which food is most similar to yours just by checking who sits closest on the map.

That coordinate [7.5, 8.0] IS an embedding. And why do engineers call it a vector? Because in geometry, when you draw an arrow from the bottom-left corner [0, 0] out to your coordinate [7.5, 8.0], that arrow is called a vector.

2. Why We Measure the Angle Between Arrows (Not Ruler Distance)

In Toy #1, we found the closest match by measuring the straight-line ruler distance (called Euclidean Distance) between the tips of the arrows.

But when we embed real text documents, ruler distance has a fatal flaw: longer documents create longer arrows.

Imagine comparing a Short Tweet about heart health ([3.0, 2.0]) against a 500-Page Medical Book on the exact same topic. Because the book has 100x more words, its arrow shoots way out across the graph.
Drag the "Book Length" slider below and watch what happens to the red Ruler Distance vs. the yellow Angle Wedge (θ):

Look at what just happened in Toy #2:

1. When you drag Book Length back and forth, the purple arrow stretches, making the Red Ruler Distance change wildly—even though the book's topic didn't change at all!
2. Meanwhile, look at the Yellow Angle Wedge (θ) tucked between the two arrows: stretching the arrow doesn't change the angle by a single degree. That is why AI databases use Cosine Similarity—it measures pure direction, ignoring document length.

3. Doing Math With Meaning: King - Man + Woman = Queen

Because every concept lives at a specific coordinate of traits, researchers discovered something wild: you can add and subtract words like numbers.

Let's plot four words on a clean 2D map where:

  • Horizontal X-Axis (Gender): Left is Masculine (-5), Center is Gender-Neutral (0), Right is Feminine (+5).
  • Vertical Y-Axis (Status): Bottom is Commoner (1), Top is Royal Monarch (9).

Look at Step 2: when you take King [-5, 9] and subtract Man [-5, 1], the horizontal Gender scores cancel out completely: -5 - (-5) = 0. That puts you right on the center neutral line at [0, 8]—a pure, genderless concept of Royalty.

Then in Step 3, adding Woman [+5, 1] pushes the coordinate to the feminine side at [+5, 9]—landing dead-on Queen.

4. From 2 Sliders to 1,536 Sliders

In our three toys above, we only used 2 sliders (X and Y) so we could draw them on a flat screen.

To map all of human language—every nuance of law, medicine, code, sarcasm, and photography—two sliders aren't enough. When you call OpenAI's embedding API, its neural network scores your input across 1,536 sliders simultaneously: 

// Calling OpenAI returns 1,536 trait scores for your sentence:
const response = await openai.embeddings.create({
  model: "text-embedding-3-small",
  input: "dog sleeping on the couch"
});

// Output: [-0.014, 0.038, -0.009, ... 1,533 more slider scores]

Even though no human can visualize a 1,536-dimensional room, the computer runs the exact same angle math from Toy #2. Whether you're searching your iPhone photos, getting song recommendations on Spotify, or giving an AI assistant long-term memory with Supabase or Pinecone, it all comes down to one simple geometric trick:

Don't match the letters. Measure the angle between the thoughts.
